## One sound, one letter

On its own, c is /t͡s/, as in Czech, Polish and Hungarian. The comma below
it, called the anchor, makes it /t͡ʃ/: <span class="ug">c̦</span>. The anchor
changes the other letters of the same family in the same way:

| Letter | <span class="ug">s</span> /s/ | <span class="ug">z</span> /z/ | <span class="ug">c</span> /t͡s/ | <span class="ug">x</span> /d͡z/ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **With the anchor** | <span class="ug">ș</span> /ʃ/ | <span class="ug">z̦</span> /ʒ/ | <span class="ug">c̦</span> /t͡ʃ/ | <span class="ug">x̦</span> /d͡ʒ/ |

## The letters

Diafon uses twenty-four Latin letters, every one but q and y: five vowels,
a e i o u, and nineteen consonants. Each letter stands for one sound.

Most consonants sound as English writes them. Four do not: c is /t͡s/, x is
/d͡z/, j is /j/ and w is /w/. These are the letters Latin spelling
wastes: c repeats k or s, x is only k plus s, and j and w are i and u
in another role.

## The marks

A small mark on a letter changes its sound. Each mark has one meaning on
consonants and one on vowels, with the exceptions listed below, and none is
taken from a national alphabet. A consonant takes one mark. A vowel takes at
most one above and one below.

### On consonants

--8<-- "marks.html"

On c and x the anchor gives the hushed pair <span class="ug">c̦ x̦</span>
/t͡ʃ d͡ʒ/, and on r the dot gives the tap <span class="ug">ṛ</span>.

The anchor is Diafon's own mark: a comma-like stroke joined to its letter,
written in one movement by hand. It sits on the thin letters too:
<span class="ug">ț ș n̦ l̦ r̦ f̦</span>.

On g and p the mark goes above, because the tail below is already taken:
<span class="ug">p̓ g̓</span>. Capitals have no tail, so the mark stays below:
<span class="ug">P̦ G̦</span>. This is the **float rule**. The double dot below
floats the same way: <span class="ug">g̈</span>, <span class="ug">G̤</span>.

### On vowels

--8<-- "vowelmarks.html"

Marks combine, at most one above and one below. The double dot and length
together make the double acute, <span class="ug">ű</span>. The dot below
takes one mark above: <span class="ug">ẹ́</span> /ɛː/,
<span class="ug">ẹ̀</span> /ɛ̃/. The double dot and the dot below together
give <span class="ug">ọ̈</span> /œ/. On r the double dot gives the vocalic r,
<span class="ug">r̈</span> /ɝ/.

## Each letter covers an area

Diafon aims to be phonemic, not phonetic. A phonetic spelling records exactly how a
sound is said. A phonemic spelling records only which sound it is, among the
sounds a language tells apart. The p in English *pin* has a puff of air and
the p in *spin* does not, but no English word depends on it, so both are p.

IPA is made for phonetics: it records exact sounds, with many symbols that are
hard to type and to read in everyday text. Diafon is for everyday writing, so
it writes only the sounds a language tells apart. This site uses IPA to say what each Diafon letter stands for.

So each letter covers a small area of sounds, and reads a little differently
from one language to the next. [Each language's table](reference.md)
shows how its letters are read.

### Consonants

Consonant areas are narrow, about one step of place or manner. For example, t is
made at the teeth in Hungarian and a little further back in English. r covers the trilled /r/ and the
English /ɹ/. <span class="ug">ṛ</span> covers both the tap and the flap,
/ɾ ɽ/, as languages hardly ever tell them apart.

The double dot below marks a language's own variant of a consonant, such as
the retroflex <span class="ug">t̤ d̤</span> of Hindi or the emphatics of
Arabic. The plain letter is a fair approximation of it. The sounds made
furthest back are written the same way: <span class="ug">k̤</span> is /q/, the
k further back, and <span class="ug">h̤</span> is /ħ/, the h further back.

Two groups are left off the chart, as each is one idea and not a place in
the mouth. The soft consonants wear the anchor:
<span class="ug">p̓ b̦ f̦ v̦ m̦ r̦ l̦ k̦ g̓</span>. Arabic's emphatic
<span class="ug">s̤ z̤</span> wear the double dot below, and its emphatic
<span class="ug">t̤ d̤</span> are the retroflex letters of the chart.

### Vowels

Vowel areas are wide. e is any front mid vowel, close or open. a is any open
vowel, front or back. ü covers /y/ and /ʏ/. Most languages have one vowel in
each area and write it bare.

A few languages have two vowels inside one area. Only they write the second
one, with a mark that means the same in all of them:
<span class="ug">ẹ ọ</span> /ɛ ɔ/ in French, Italian, Portuguese, Hindi,
Thai and Vietnamese; <span class="ug">ị</span> /ɪ/ in Dutch and Ukrainian;
<span class="ug">ä</span> /æ/ in Finnish, Estonian and Persian. A language
that makes the split writes it everywhere, in long, nasal and diphthong
vowels too: French /ɔ/ is <span class="ug">ọ</span> in *porte* and
<span class="ug">ọ̀</span> in *bon*.

When long and short vowels also differ in quality, length is enough. German
*Ofen* is <span class="ug">ó</span> /oː/ and *offen* is o /ɔ/, so German
needs no split. English is the same: *bit* i, *beat* í.

The schwa has its own area, <span class="ug">ạ</span> /ə/. It sits next to
both a and e in too many languages to belong to either.

Two sounds that are one phoneme in a language share one letter: English /ʌ/
is its stressed schwa, <span class="ug">ạ</span> ([rule 3](reference.md#rule-3),
one phoneme, one letter).

<span class="ug">ä</span> is made like <span class="ug">ü</span> and
<span class="ug">ö</span>, but it is a split inside the a area: any
language with a front rounded vowel writes <span class="ug">ü</span> or
<span class="ug">ö</span>, while <span class="ug">ä</span> is written only by a
language with both a front and a back a. Everywhere else a is a.

### The limit

An area is kept small enough that its sounds are not easily told apart: no u for /y/, no
voiced for voiceless. Dialects and fine detail are not written, as in any
everyday spelling.

## Length, capitals and tones

A long vowel takes an acute, <span class="ug">á</span>, and a long
consonant is written double, <span class="ug">șș</span>. Aspiration is
written with h, as it sounds: <span class="ug">th</span> /tʰ/.

### Capitals

Capitals belong to spelling and leave the sound as it is:
<span class="ug">Șip</span>, <span class="ug">ȘIP</span>. A long consonant
capitalises only its first letter, <span class="ug">Șș</span>.

### Tones

A tone is written as a sign after the syllable, and each sign is the same
tone in each language. The Diafon fonts draw it as a mark above the
syllable: <span class="ug">maˉ maˊ maˇ maˋ</span>.

| Tone | high | rising | dipping | falling | mid | low | glottal rising | glottal low |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sign | <span class="ug">ˉ</span> | <span class="ug">ˊ</span> | <span class="ug">ˇ</span> | <span class="ug">ˋ</span> | none | <span class="ug">ˍ</span> | <span class="ug">˜</span> | <span class="ug">ˀ</span> |

Mid tone has no mark, as in Thai and Vietnamese spelling. The mark sits
well clear of the vowel's own marks: one letter wide over a one-letter
syllable, two over a longer one, with a small gap after the syllable that
shows where it ends. The low and the glottal tones stay after the syllable.

In the text a tone is stored as a sign after its syllable, the Bopomofo tone
marks in the table, and the Diafon fonts draw it above. Other fonts, or the
Diafon fonts with contextual alternates turned off, show the sign after the
syllable. A language's own spelling can still show tones its usual way,
like the accents of Pinyin.

## Text and fonts

Diafon text is ordinary Unicode, so any app, file or message can carry it.
Each letter is a plain Latin letter plus standard combining marks:

| Mark | Character | On g and p |
|---|---|---|
| anchor | U+0326 comma below | U+0313 comma above |
| dot | U+0323 dot below | U+0307 dot above |
| double dot below | U+0324 diaeresis below | U+0308 diaeresis above |
| acute, grave, double acute | U+0301, U+0300, U+030B | |
| double dot | U+0308 | |
| dot below a vowel | U+0323 | |
| tone signs | U+02C9 ˉ, U+02CA ˊ, U+02C7 ˇ, U+02CB ˋ, U+02CD ˍ, U+02DC ˜, U+02C0 ˀ | |

Text is stored in the standard composed form (NFC). Where Unicode has a
ready-made letter (<span class="ug">ș ṭ ä á</span>) that letter is used.
Elsewhere the letter and its mark stay a pair (<span class="ug">t̤ k̦ a̋</span>).
Both count as the same text.

The Diafon fonts (Serif, Sans and Mono) draw the marks as designed, with the
anchor joined to its letter. Other fonts draw the same characters roughly in
place, and the font picker on the [Reference](reference.md) page shows how any
font does.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| mark | a small sign on a letter that changes its sound in a set way |
| anchor | the comma-like mark joined below a consonant |
| double dot below | two dots under a consonant: a language's own variant, further back |
| float rule | on g and p the mark goes above |
| area | the range of sounds one letter covers |
| split | a second vowel inside an area, written only by languages that have both |

Diafon is named after the diaphoneme: one unit for a sound that several
languages share, even where each says it a little differently.
