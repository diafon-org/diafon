## The letters

Diafon uses twenty-four of the Latin letters, all but q and y. Five are
vowels, a e i o u, and nineteen are consonants. Each letter stands for one
sound.

Most consonants sound as they do in English. Four do not.

| Letter | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| <span class="ug">c</span> | /t͡s/ | Czech *cena*, <span class="ug">cena</span> |
| <span class="ug">x</span> | /d͡z/ | Polish *dzwon*, <span class="ug">xvon</span> |
| <span class="ug">j</span> | /j/ | English *yes*, <span class="ug">jes</span> |
| <span class="ug">w</span> | /w/ | English *wet*, <span class="ug">wet</span> |

Most spellings waste c and x. c repeats k or s, and x is k followed by s.
Diafon gives each of them a sound that has no other letter. j and w are the
consonants that go with i and u.

## The marks

A mark on a letter changes its sound. The same mark changes every letter in
the same way, so a letter you have not met can still be read.

Take the comma below, called the anchor. <span class="ug">s</span> is /s/ and <span class="ug">ș</span> is /ʃ/. The
anchor does the same to z, c and x.

| Plain | <span class="ug">s</span> /s/ | <span class="ug">z</span> /z/ | <span class="ug">c</span> /t͡s/ | <span class="ug">x</span> /d͡z/ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **With the anchor** | <span class="ug">ș</span> /ʃ/ | <span class="ug">z̦</span> /ʒ/ | <span class="ug">c̦</span> /t͡ʃ/ | <span class="ug">x̦</span> /d͡ʒ/ |

There are three marks for consonants and three for vowels. A consonant takes
one mark. A vowel takes at most one above and one below.

### On consonants

--8<-- "marks.html"

On r the dot is the tap, <span class="ug">ṛ</span> /ɾ/.

The anchor is Diafon's own mark. It is a comma-like stroke joined to its
letter, and by hand the two are written in one movement. Thin letters take
it too: <span class="ug">ț ș n̦ l̦ r̦ f̦</span>.

g and p already have a tail below, so their mark goes above: <span class="ug">p̓ g̓</span>.
Capitals have no tail, and the mark stays below: <span class="ug">P̦ G̦</span>. This is the
**float rule**. The dot and the double dot below move the same way: <span class="ug">ġ</span>,
<span class="ug">g̈</span>, <span class="ug">G̤</span>.

### On vowels

--8<-- "vowelmarks.html"

A vowel takes the double dot or the dot below, never both. Either can be
long as well. The double dot and length together are the double acute,
<span class="ug">ű</span>. A dotted long vowel takes the acute, <span class="ug">ọ́</span> /ɔː/.

A nasal vowel has no mark of its own. It is written as the vowel followed
by <span class="ug">ṃ</span>, m with the dot. <span class="ug">ṃ</span> has no sound itself and only makes the vowel
before it nasal. French *bon* is <span class="ug">boṃ</span> and Hindi *hā̃* is <span class="ug">haṃ</span>.

Aspiration is written the same way, with h. <span class="ug">th</span> is an aspirated t, /tʰ/.

### The double dot on consonants

Four consonants take the double dot above.

- <span class="ug">r̈</span> /ɝ/ is the r that is a vowel, as in English *bird*, <span class="ug">br̈d</span>.
- <span class="ug">ẅ</span> /ɥ/ is the glide that goes with ü, as j goes with i and w with u.
- <span class="ug">m̈</span> and <span class="ug">n̈</span> are a nasal that is a syllable in front of its own letter.
  Igbo *mmadụ* is <span class="ug">m̈madụ</span>, said m-ma-dụ.

Without the mark, a doubled letter is always one long consonant, as in
Italian <span class="ug">mamma</span>.

## Length, capitals and tones

A long vowel takes the acute, <span class="ug">á</span>. A long consonant is written twice,
<span class="ug">șș</span>.

### Capitals

Capitals follow the spelling habits of each language and do not change the
sound: <span class="ug">Șip</span>, <span class="ug">ȘIP</span>. A long consonant capitalises only its first letter,
<span class="ug">Șș</span>.

### Tones

A tone is written with a sign after its syllable. A sign means the same
tone in every language.

| Tone | high | rising | dipping | falling | mid | low | glottal rising | glottal low |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sign | <span class="ug">ˉ</span> | <span class="ug">ˊ</span> | <span class="ug">ˇ</span> | <span class="ug">ˋ</span> | none | <span class="ug">ˍ</span> | <span class="ug">˜</span> | <span class="ug">ˀ</span> |

The mid tone has no sign, as in Thai and Vietnamese spelling.

The Diafon fonts draw the first four signs above the syllable:
<span class="ug">maˉ maˊ maˇ maˋ</span>. The sign sits clear of the vowel's own marks. It is one
letter wide, or two over a longer syllable, and a small gap after the
syllable shows where it ends. The low and the two glottal signs stay after
the syllable.

Other fonts show every sign after its syllable. So do the Diafon fonts when
contextual alternates are turned off.

A language's usual spelling keeps its own way of showing tones, like the
accents of Pinyin.

## Each letter covers an area

Diafon is phonemic. It writes the sounds a language tells apart and leaves
out finer detail. The p of English *pin* has a puff of air and the p of
*spin* has none. No English word depends on the difference, so both are p.

IPA does the other job. It records exact sounds, and for that it needs many
symbols that are hard to type and slow to read. This site uses IPA only to
say what each Diafon letter stands for.

So a letter covers a small area of sounds, and is said a little differently
from one language to the next. The [Reference](reference.md) shows how each
language reads its letters.

### Consonants

A consonant's area is narrow. t is made at the teeth in French and a
little further back in English. r is the trilled /r/ and also the English
/ɹ/. <span class="ug">ṛ</span> is both the tap and the flap, /ɾ ɽ/, which languages hardly ever
tell apart.

Some languages need more letters and make them with the marks. These are
not on the chart. One such letter can serve two languages in two ways:
<span class="ug">t̤ d̤</span> are retroflex in Hindi and emphatic in Arabic. Read as the plain
letter, each is still close.

### Vowels

A vowel's area is wide. e is any front mid vowel, close or open. a is any
open vowel, front or back. ü is /y/ and /ʏ/. Most languages have one vowel
in each area and write it with the plain letter.

A few languages have two vowels in one area. They write the more open one
with the dot below.

- <span class="ug">ẹ ọ</span> /ɛ ɔ/ in French, Italian, Portuguese, Bengali, Vietnamese and Thai
- <span class="ug">ị</span> /ɪ/ in Dutch and Ukrainian
- <span class="ug">ị ọ ụ</span> /ɪ ɔ ʊ/ in Igbo, whose usual spelling marks them the same way

Finnish, Estonian and Persian have a front a beside the back one. The front
one is <span class="ug">ä</span> /æ/, with the double dot as on <span class="ug">ö</span> and <span class="ug">ü</span>.

A language that splits an area keeps the mark in its long vowels and
diphthongs. Thai /ɔ/ is <span class="ug">ọ</span> short and <span class="ug">ọ́</span> long.

Where a long and a short vowel also differ in quality, length is enough.
German *Ofen* has <span class="ug">ó</span> /oː/ and *offen* has <span class="ug">o</span> /ɔ/, so German needs no dot.
English is the same: *bit* has <span class="ug">i</span> and *beat* has <span class="ug">í</span>.

The schwa /ə/ is <span class="ug">ë</span>, as in English *about*, German *bitte* and French
*le*. Mandarin and Korean write their own mid vowel with the same letter.

Two sounds that are one phoneme in a language share a letter. English /ʌ/
is its stressed schwa, so it is <span class="ug">ë</span> too
([rule 3](reference.md#rule-3)).

### The limit

An area stays small enough that its sounds are hard to tell apart. u never
stands for /y/, and a voiced letter never stands for a voiceless sound.
Dialects and fine detail are not written, as in any everyday spelling.

## Text and fonts

Diafon text is ordinary Unicode, so any app, file or message can carry it.
A letter is a plain Latin letter with standard combining marks.

| Mark | Character | On g and p |
|---|---|---|
| anchor | U+0326 comma below | U+0313 comma above |
| dot | U+0323 dot below | U+0307 dot above |
| double dot below | U+0324 diaeresis below | U+0308 diaeresis above |
| acute, double acute | U+0301, U+030B | |
| double dot | U+0308 | |
| dot below a vowel | U+0323 | |
| tone signs | U+02C9 ˉ, U+02CA ˊ, U+02C7 ˇ, U+02CB ˋ, U+02CD ˍ, U+02DC ˜, U+02C0 ˀ | |

Text is stored in the standard composed form, NFC. Where Unicode has a
letter ready-made, that one is used: <span class="ug">ș ṭ ä á</span>. Otherwise the letter and
its mark stay two characters: <span class="ug">t̤ k̦ a̋</span>. Both count as the same text.

The Diafon fonts, Serif, Sans and Mono, draw the marks as designed, with
the anchor joined to its letter. Other fonts place the same characters
roughly. The [font check](font.md#check-a-font) shows how any font does.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| mark | a small sign on a letter that changes its sound |
| anchor | the comma-like mark joined below a consonant |
| double dot below | two dots under a consonant, for a sound made further back |
| float rule | on g and p a mark goes above the letter |
| area | the range of sounds one letter covers |
| split | a second vowel in one area, written only by the languages that have both |

Diafon is named after the diaphoneme, a single unit for a sound that
several languages share and each says a little differently.
