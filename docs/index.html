Diaphone

One keyboard, one script, every language — a universal phonemic writing and input system.

What this is

Diaphone is a universal phonemic writing system with a matching input system. It has two faces:

  • The input form — what you type. Plain letters from the minimal Latin set, on any ordinary keyboard, one layout worldwide.
  • The script form — what you read. Latin base letters carrying a small set of characteristic marks, one glyph per point of the system.

The input form translates into the script form unambiguously: no dead keys, no candidate menus, no guessing.

Cornerstones

Two commitments drive every other decision:

  • One keyboard. The input uses only the minimal Latin set — the 26 lowercase letters — so a single mobile keyboard serves every language. Each language annotates its own phonemic distinctions within that one set.
  • One small, learnable point set. A universal phonemic set of points, deliberately small, rendered smoothly as Latin base characters plus a few marks — one coherent writing system for multilingual people and language learners across many languages.

Two consequences the system embraces up front:

  • Phonemic, not phonetic. The system records what a language contrasts, never how a sound is precisely realised. It therefore does not follow existing transcription conventions — those are mostly language-specific or phonetic, and Diaphone is deliberately neither.
  • A point is not a fixed sound everywhere. A small set covering many languages means most points cannot carry one fixed phonetic value. Diaphone makes this graded and explicit — see the bands below.

Typing: bases and operators

The 26 Latin letters are split into two disjoint roles:

  • Base characters carry a point on their own.
  • Operator characters never stand alone; they modify the preceding base.

A point is typed as a base plus zero or more operators. This separation is what makes the input provably unambiguous: an operator can never be read as the start of a new point, and a base always starts one.

The partition itself is forced, not chosen. Twenty letters carry a widespread phoneme that no other letter covers:

RoleLetters
vowel basesa e i o u
consonant basesb d f g h k l m n p r s t v z
operatorsc j q x w y

The six operators are exactly the redundant letters of the Latin alphabet: c and q duplicate k or s, x spells the cluster k+s, and j, y, w name glides — compositions of the close vowels i and u. Making any of them a base would waste a letter on a duplicate value; making any base an operator would rob a common phoneme of its letter. Six is also the necessary number: the system needs six mutually independent operator meanings, each pair held apart by a language that contrasts what a smaller set would merge — Ukrainian ч ~ ць keeps the hush affricate distinct from affricate-plus-palatal, Korean /t͈/ ~ /tʰ/ keeps length distinct from the exotic slot. Between the two beyond-regular letters the roles divide by direction: x is the fricative shift (kx preserves the letter that names /x/), while y leans back — retroflex and the deep points — and pairs into the special. The palatal seat itself belongs to j (tj is Hungarian ty), so ty cannot be mistaken for it.

So the exotic band covers vowels by the same mechanism as consonants, and nasality is not a separate concept — it is the vowel exotic's default reading. The exotic does not thin the vowel point set, though: the rare front-rounded pair /œ ʏ/ cannot ride it, because French holds both /œ/ and nasal vowels and one slot never carries two sounds within one language — so they stay ordinary q-points.

Tones need no operator: plain trailing digits, in input and script alike (șan1); a language's own spelling target may render them natively, as Pinyin does with diacritics.

The whole input has a hard boundary: a form carries at most two operators — length's w rides free on top — so no segment costs more than three keystrokes. Any operator can serve as the secondary, its reading set by the one before it, exactly as first operators read by their base.

The two bands

Every point belongs to one of two bands, describing how widely its phonetic value may swing across languages:

BandWhat it coversIts law
regular the point inventory — bases and operator points a point's per-language readings stay in its cell of the point table, drifting at most into a neighbouring column — adjacent place for a consonant, an adjacent quality step for a vowel
exotic the special (yx) a systemic modifier (aspiration, velarisation, retroflexion, …) read per language against a stated default — wider than any regular point, never an arbitrary one-off sound, always in the neighbourhood of its base

There is no sharp line between "fixed" and "approximate" points inside the regular band — a p is the same everywhere, a t may be dental here and alveolar there — and the system does not pretend one exists: the neighbouring-column law is the whole guarantee.

Entering a phoneme on a slot the language does not contrast needs one of two licences. The exotic licence: a quality most outside speakers cannot produce anyway — implosives, ejectives, emphatics, the special's whole domain — may overload its base form, because an outsider's approximation is exactly what is written. The minimal-move licence: everywhere else the drift must be perceptually minor, keeping a cross-language reader's default pronunciation relatively precise — one height step (e for ɛ), dental for alveolar, aspiration on a voiceless stop, rounding in the open region (a for ɒ). Neither licence ever covers a miss a listener would flag: no u for y, no voicing or manner swaps.

Dialect and speech impediments are never recorded. The exotic notation may be borrowed for such cases — it is rare otherwise — but no new notation is ever invented for them. Very fine phonetic qualities stay outside the notation entirely, as they do in every practical orthography; Diaphone remains significantly more expressive than current practice in the majority of cases.

The marks

The script form is always built on small letters. A consonant wears its mark below, governed by the float rule: every mark floats above when its letter has a descender. A vowel wears at most one mark above and one below; two marks never stack above — the double acute exists precisely because flip + long would otherwise have to. Marks, like operators, read per letter class.

The signature consonant mark of the system is the anchor: a comma-like stroke that is both elongated and attached to its base letter. This is deliberate — the attached form is not in wide use anywhere, so it can become Diaphone's visual signature, and the attachment gives handwriting an easy, distinctive gesture: one continuous stroke flowing off the letter. Approximated with standard combining marks until the Diaphone font carries the true attached form: ț ș n̦ — and by the placement rule, g wears its anchor on top.

Not every point composes from marks — the operator letters themselves take script-form jobs. c is /t͡s/ and x is /d͡z/; the anchor turns them into the hush affricates c̦ /t͡ʃ/ and x̦ /d͡ʒ/ — so the anchor marks the whole hush family: ș z̦ c̦ x̦. q is the glottal stop /ʔ/ (its value in Maltese), and with its mark — above, since q has a descender — the uvular: q̇ /q/. The glides take j /j/ and w /w/. And length needs no consonant mark at all: a long consonant doubles its letter.

The vowel triangle

All seventeen vowel points. Front on the left, back on the right, height falling toward the open apex; the flipped forms sit beside their partners, the lowered forms one step down, the reduced ạ at the centre.

Point is chosen over unit, sign and diaphone: it is short and composes well (point set, point inventory, a point's band). The system's name still honours the concept — a point is exactly what linguistics calls a diaphoneme, one abstract unit uniting corresponding phonemes across languages, realised differently by each.
