What a "point" is

A point is the bare skeleton of a sound — nothing extra. For a consonant it's just three facts: where in the mouth (place), how the air is blocked (manner), and whether the voice is on or off (voicing). Two consonants from different languages are the same point when those facts match.

Anything else — holding it longer, sending air through the nose, adding a puff or a "y" colour — is a modifier that rides on top. Modifiers never make a new point; they decorate one. That's the whole trick: a handful of points, plus a few modifiers, instead of hundreds of one-off symbols.

A consonant point = place · manner · voice

Place = where the blockage is (lips → throat). Manner = how you block the air (full stop, hissy fricative, nasal…). Voice = are the vocal cords buzzing (b) or silent (p). The grid below is laid out on exactly these axes.
