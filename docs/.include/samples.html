EnglishThe North Wind and the Sun (Aesop)

Ḍạ Nórṭ Wind ạnd ḍạ Sạn wr̈ dispjútiṇ wic̦ wáz ḍạ stróṇr̈, wen ạ travạlr̈ kejm ạlóṇ rapt in ạ wórm klowk. Ḍej ạgríd ḍat ḍạ wạn hú fr̈st sạksídid in mejkiṇ ḍạ travạlr̈ tejk hiz klowk óf șud bí kạnsidr̈d stróṇr̈ ḍan ḍạ ạḍr̈. Ḍen ḍạ Nórṭ Wind blú az hárd az hí kud, bạt ḍạ mór hí blú ḍạ mór klowslí did ḍạ travạlr̈ fowld hiz klowk r̈awnd him; ạnd at last ḍạ Nórṭ Wind gejv ạp ḍạ ạtempt. Ḍen ḍạ Sạn șajnd awt wórmlí, ạnd imídíạtlí ḍạ travạlr̈ tuk óf hiz klowk. Ạnd sow ḍạ Nórṭ Wind wáz ạblajx̦d tú kạnfes ḍat ḍạ Sạn wáz ḍạ stróṇr̈ ạv ḍạ tú.

The North Wind and the Sun were disputing which was the stronger, when a traveler came along wrapped in a warm cloak. They agreed that the one who first succeeded in making the traveler take his cloak off should be considered stronger than the other. Then the North Wind blew as hard as he could, but the more he blew the more closely did the traveler fold his cloak around him; and at last the North Wind gave up the attempt. Then the Sun shined out warmly, and immediately the traveler took off his cloak. And so the North Wind was obliged to confess that the Sun was the stronger of the two.

EnglishSonnet 18 (Shakespeare)

Șal Aj kạmper ḍí tú ạ sạmr̈z dej?
Ḍaw árt mór lạvlí ạnd mór temprạt:
Rạf windz dú șejk ḍạ dárliṇ bạdz ạv Mej,
Ạnd sạmr̈z lís haṭ ól tú șórt ạ dejt;
Sạmtajm tú hát ḍạ aj ạv hevạn șajnz,
Ạnd ófạn iz hiz gowld kạmplekșạn dimm'd;
Ạnd evr̈í fer frạm fer sạmtajm diklajnz,
Baj c̦ans ór nejc̦r̈z c̦ejnx̦iṇ kórs untrimm'd.

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
And summer's lease hath all too short a date;
Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
And often is his gold complexion dimm'd;
And every fair from fair sometime declines,
By chance or nature's changing course untrimm'd.
