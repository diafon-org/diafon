## Spelling rules

Diafon writes the sounds each language tells apart, so one sound can take a
different letter in two languages. The first three rules cover every such
row in the tables, and the row names its rule. The fourth is about
syllables.

<div class="rules" markdown="0">
<p id="rule-1"><b>1. Hushed and soft sounds.</b> A language's hushed sounds take the anchor,
<span class="ug">ș z̦ c̦ x̦</span>, wherever exactly the tongue is. English /ʃ/, Mandarin /ʂ/ and Japanese /ɕ/ are
all <span class="ug">ș</span>. Some languages have a second, softer series beside them and write it with the dot,
<span class="ug">ṣ ẓ c̣ x̣</span>. Polish sz and ś are <span class="ug">ș</span> and <span class="ug">ṣ</span>, Russian ш and сь are <span class="ug">ș</span> and <span class="ug">ṣ</span>, and
Ukrainian ч and ць are <span class="ug">c̦</span> and <span class="ug">c̣</span>.</p>
<p id="rule-2"><b>2. A vowel area splits only where a language needs it.</b> A plain vowel
covers an area. A language with two vowels in one area marks the more open one with the
dot below, <span class="ug">ẹ ọ ị ụ</span>, and the front a with the double dot, <span class="ug">ä</span>. It keeps the mark in its
long vowels. Where length already tells two vowels apart, there is no mark. English /iː/
and /ɪ/ are <span class="ug">í</span> and <span class="ug">i</span>. Dutch /i/ and /ɪ/ are <span class="ug">i</span> and <span class="ug">ị</span>.</p>
<p id="rule-3"><b>3. One phoneme, one letter.</b> Two sounds that are one phoneme in a
language share the letter of the main one. English /ʌ/ is its stressed schwa, <span class="ug">ë</span>.
Mandarin /ə/ and /ɤ/ are one vowel, <span class="ug">ë</span> as well.</p>
<p id="rule-4"><b>4. A consonant can be a syllable.</b> A nasal, an l or an r with no
vowel beside it is a syllable of its own and takes no mark. Swahili <span class="ug">mtu</span> is m-tu, and
Czech <span class="ug">vlk</span> has its l at the centre. A syllabic nasal in front of its own letter takes
the double dot above, <span class="ug">m̈ n̈</span>. Igbo <span class="ug">m̈madụ</span> is m-ma-dụ and Swahili <span class="ug">n̈ne</span> is n-ne. So a
doubled letter is one long consonant in every language: Italian <span class="ug">mamma</span>, English
<span class="ug">howmmejd</span>. Where tones are written, a syllabic consonant's tone sign follows it as it
follows any syllable.</p>
</div>
